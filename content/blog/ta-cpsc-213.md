After teaching CPSC 110 for two terms, and since I happened to be taking courses over the summer, I decided to sign up to become a TA for CPSC 213, my first low-level programming course and one that I really enjoyed taking with Dr. K during the winter term.

To give a brief overview of the course, CPSC 213 (Introduction to Computer Systems) is an introductory computer systems course that feels quite different from other higher-level courses such as CPSC 110 (Functional Programming/Racket), CPSC 210 (OOP/OOD/Java), and CPSC 221 (Data Structures and Algorithms).

Being a low-level programming course, CPSC 213 requires a shift in focus from mastering syntax, design patterns, and implementation to understanding what actually happens behind the scenes when a program runs. This involves more conceptual understanding, discussions, and textbook readings rather than simply learning how to implement something.

I quickly developed an interest in the course because it offered me a different way of looking at programming. Instead of just focusing on whether my code works, I started thinking more about how it works, how the computer executes it, and how different implementation decisions can affect performance and efficiency. It also introduced me to the idea that almost everything in programming has room for optimization, which I found particularly interesting and which later helped me build a solid foundation for the optimization work I've been involved in since then.

## How My Teaching Style Changed from CPSC 110 to CPSC 213

Having been a CPSC 110 TA previously, I quickly realized that I needed to adapt my teaching style when I became a CPSC 213 TA. In CPSC 213, misconceptions are often the biggest blockers for students being introduced to lower-level programming languages for the first time. For example, treating C as Java with just different syntax, confusing stack and heap memory, or struggling to distinguish pointers from the values they point to, especially when pointers get moved around or when dealing with pointers to pointers. These misconceptions can make even relatively simple concepts difficult to understand without a solid foundation in how memory actually works.

While CPSC 110 places a strong emphasis on following systematic design recipes and developing problem-solving skills, I found that teaching CPSC 213 requires a slightly different approach, one that I really enjoy using and have seen other TAs use as well.

Instead of immediately pointing out mistakes, we first try to understand where the misconceptions come from. From there, we ask guiding questions such as, "What you described works in this scenario, but what about this slightly different scenario I've drawn?" or "What do you think happens to this pointer after the function returns?"

By introducing small changes to the original problem and encouraging students to reason through them, we help them identify gaps in their own understanding rather than simply giving them the correct answer. Through teaching CPSC 213, I've come to appreciate how important it is to challenge assumptions, not just when learning C, but throughout software engineering. As we gain experience with different languages, frameworks, and tools that operate at different levels of abstraction, being able to question what we think we know becomes increasingly valuable.
