Last spring, I had the opportunity to TA for CPSC 110, an introductory computer science course at UBC. The application process was straightforward: I applied online with a recommendation from my lab TA, Sean, along with a personal statement explaining why I wanted to TA for the course. I listed CPSC 110 as my top choice, so I was thrilled when I received the offer.

## About CPSC 110

CPSC 110 is a required course for students majoring in Computer Science and related programs, such as BUCS and COGS. Across the 2025 Winter Terms 1 and 2, approximately 2,000 students were enrolled in the course across all sections.

The course introduces many fundamental computer science concepts using Racket, a functional programming language and a dialect of Lisp (interestingly, Waterloo's CS135 is another foundational CS course taught using Racket). Key concepts covered in the course include the How to Design Functions (HtDF) and How to Design Data (HtDD) design recipes, recursion, and parameterization.

What makes CPSC 110 particularly interesting is that it also introduces foundational algorithms and data structures, including graphs, binary search trees (BSTs), depth-first search (DFS), and breadth-first search (BFS). Many of these topics are revisited in greater depth in CPSC 221. The course also introduces higher-order functions such as foldr, map, and filter, concepts that reappear in CPSC 213 when discussing abstraction and functional programming.

## What TAing taught me about learning and teaching

### 1. Structure is what makes hard things learnable

The design recipes taught me that good teaching isn't about showing students the answer; it's about giving them a repeatable process for finding answers themselves. Beginners rarely struggle because a concept is too hard. They struggle because they don't know where to start, and a well-designed scaffold turns a blank page into a series of small, answerable questions. I've started applying this to my own learning: when something feels impossible, the real problem is usually that I haven't broken it down yet.

### 2. You don't understand something until you've watched someone misunderstand it

Explaining recursion to one student is easy. Explaining it to thirty students who each misunderstand it differently is where real understanding begins. Every lab and office hour exposed gaps in my own knowledge that acing the course never did, because teaching forces you to rebuild ideas from first principles instead of leaning on the intuitions that got you through the exam. If you want to learn something deeply, put yourself in a position where you have to teach it.

### 3. Teach ideas, not tools

Students often measure a course by how marketable its language is, and "no one uses Racket in industry" was the most common complaint I heard. But the best courses teach ideas that outlive any particular tool. Racket will fade from most students' memories within a term; thinking in terms of data design, decomposition, and abstraction won't. As a TA, I learned to redirect "why are we learning this?" away from the syntax and toward the ideas underneath it. Education isn't about accumulating tools, it's about building ways of thinking that transfer to whatever tool comes next.
